# Annabel Lee
A poem by Edgar Allan Poe
1849 · A love the grave could not end

## Read the Poem

It was many and many a year ago,
In a kingdom by the sea,
That a maiden there lived whom you may know
By the name of Annabel Lee;
And this maiden she lived with no other thought
Than to love and be loved by me.

I was a child and she was a child,
In this kingdom by the sea,
But we loved with a love that was more than love—
I and my Annabel Lee—
With a love that the wingèd seraphs of Heaven
Coveted her and me.

And this was the reason that, long ago,
In this kingdom by the sea,
A wind blew out of a cloud, chilling
My beautiful Annabel Lee;
So that her highborn kinsmen came
And bore her away from me,
To shut her up in a sepulchre
In this kingdom by the sea.

The angels, not half so happy in Heaven,
Went envying her and me—
Yes!—that was the reason (as all men know,
In this kingdom by the sea)
That the wind came out of the cloud by night,
Chilling and killing my Annabel Lee.

But our love it was stronger by far than the love
Of those who were older than we—
Of many far wiser than we—
And neither the angels in Heaven above
Nor the demons down under the sea
Can ever dissever my soul from the soul
Of the beautiful Annabel Lee;

For the moon never beams, without bringing me dreams
Of the beautiful Annabel Lee;
And the stars never rise, but I feel the bright eyes
Of the beautiful Annabel Lee;
And so, all the night-tide, I lie down by the side
Of my darling—my darling—my life and my bride,
In her sepulchre there by the sea—
In her tomb by the sounding sea.

## Author

Summary: Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) was an American writer, poet, editor, and critic, and one of the central figures of American Romanticism and Gothic literature.

Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) was an American writer, poet, editor, and critic, and one of the central figures of American Romanticism and Gothic literature. He was orphaned young and raised by John Allan, a Richmond merchant. Poe is generally credited as the first well-known American author who tried to support himself entirely through writing and editing, which kept him in near-constant financial hardship. He married his young cousin, Virginia Clemm, in 1836; her long, slow loss to tuberculosis in 1847 devastated him. Poe himself died in Baltimore in 1849 under mysterious circumstances. "Annabel Lee" is considered the last poem he completed, written in the months before he died and published two days after his death.

## Time, Era, and Societal Context

Summary: Romantic writers of this period prized intense emotion, the individual imagination, mystery, and the sublime over Enlightenment-era reason and order.

Literary movement: American Romanticism / Dark Romanticism (Gothic). Romantic writers of this period prized intense emotion, the individual imagination, mystery, and the sublime over Enlightenment-era reason and order.

Poe believed poetry didn't need to teach a moral lesson — its purpose was to create a single, unified emotional effect in the reader. He anticipated the "art for art's sake" movement by attacking what he called "the heresy of the Didactic."

Historical backdrop: 1849 America was expanding westward, and the country was increasingly divided over slavery. Poe's poem pointedly ignores all of this — it is set in a vague, fairy-tale "kingdom by the sea," which lets it float free of any specific place or political moment.

Biographical backdrop: Poe wrote it not long after losing his wife Virginia. Grief over these losses is widely believed to have shaped the poem, even though scholars still debate exactly who (if anyone specific) "Annabel Lee" represents.

## Summary (Characters, Setting, Plot)

Summary: The poem tells the story of a man who loved a maiden named Annabel Lee in a kingdom by the sea, whose death was caused by the envy of angels.

Speaker/Narrator: An unnamed man, the former lover of Annabel Lee, now grieving her death.

Annabel Lee: A maiden the speaker loved since childhood; she dies young.

Setting: A vague, legendary "kingdom by the sea" — deliberately timeless and placeless, like a fairy tale.

Plot: Many years ago, in a kingdom by the sea, a maiden named Annabel Lee lived with no thought except loving and being loved by the speaker. Their love was so complete that even winged angels in heaven envied it. Because of that envy, a wind blew out of a cloud one night and chilled Annabel Lee, and her highborn relatives came and shut her away in a tomb by the sea. The speaker insists that neither angels above nor demons below the sea can ever separate his soul from hers. Every night, he says, the moon and stars bring him dreams and visions of her eyes, and he lies down beside her in her tomb by the sea.

## Analysis (Salient Points)

Summary: The poem is a lyric elegy — it's less about narrating an event and more about revealing the speaker's emotional (and increasingly unstable) state.

The poem is a lyric elegy — it's less about narrating an event and more about revealing the speaker's emotional (and increasingly unstable) state.

The speaker never accepts a natural explanation (illness) for Annabel Lee's death; he reframes it as cosmic jealousy, which is a way of giving unbearable, random loss a dramatic, meaningful cause.

The poem doesn't resolve neatly into "healthy mourning" or "unhealthy obsession" — critics note that it deliberately keeps this ambiguous. The final image can be read as either the ultimate proof of eternal love or a picture of someone who cannot let go and has stopped living his own life.

Modern readers sometimes discuss the poem alongside the stages of grief (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance), though this is a modern psychological lens, not a scholarly consensus.

## Major Themes and Motifs

Summary: Love that survives death, grief and the refusal to let go, and cosmic injustice are the major themes developed through motifs.

Love that survives death: Repeated insistence that no earthly or heavenly power can "dissever" their souls.

Grief and the refusal to let go: Motif of nightly visitation to the tomb; recurring dreams of her eyes in the stars.

Envy of the divine / cosmic injustice: The recurring accusation against "the angels" and "winged seraphs."

Idealized, childlike love: Repetition of "I was a child and she was a child."

The sea as boundary between life and death: The recurring refrain "kingdom by the sea" and "sepulchre there by the sea."

Key recurring motif — the refrain: The repetition of "Annabel Lee" and "kingdom by the sea" functions like a chant or incantation, reinforcing how fixated the speaker is on both the woman and the place where he lost her.

## Major Symbols

Summary: The sea, the wind, angels, the tomb, and the moon and stars serve as the major symbols in the poem.

The sea: Represents vastness, isolation, and the boundary between the world of the living and the world of the dead.

The wind/cloud that kills her: A symbol for sudden, arbitrary illness or mortality (echoing the long, slow loss of his wife to tuberculosis).

Angels/"winged seraphs": Symbolize both divine judgment and — in the speaker's paranoid logic — divine jealousy of human happiness.

The tomb/sepulchre: Symbolizes both the finality of death and the speaker's refusal to accept that finality.

The moon and stars: Symbolize memory and the persistence of the beloved's presence in the speaker's mind.

## Important Conventions

Summary: The poem functions as an elegy and a ballad, utilizing the &quot;death of a beautiful woman&quot; convention.

The professor's list (classical unities, noble birth, tragic flaw, free will) was written for drama, so it only partly fits two poems and a short story. Here is how they apply to "Annabel Lee":

Elegy: It is a poem of mourning for a dead loved one, which makes it the poem's main convention.

Ballad: It has the repeated refrain, the simple story told in verse, and the musical rhythm. Though it is not technically a ballad, Poe referred to it as one.

Death of a beautiful woman: This is Poe's signature convention. In "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846), he called the death of a beautiful woman "the most poetical topic in the world."

Dark Romantic setting: The setting is a timeless, fairy-tale "kingdom by the sea," with a tomb, angels and demons.

Noble birth (partly): Annabel Lee's "highborn kinsmen" suggest she comes from a noble family. They're the ones who carry her away.

Free will vs. fate: The speaker blames forces outside human control, the envious angels and the wind, rather than chance or illness.

Doesn't apply: Tragic flaw and the classical unities.

## How the Text Resonates Today

Summary: Grief that refuses closure is a universally recognized experience, often discussed in modern psychology.

Grief that refuses closure is a universally recognized experience. Modern readers and critics still discuss where "devoted love" ends and "obsession" begins — a very contemporary conversation around grief, attachment, and mental health.

The poem continues to be adapted across music, film, and other media well over a century later, showing how enduring the universal ache of losing someone young feels across generations.

# The Cask of Amontillado
A story by Edgar Allan Poe
1846 · The perfect, unpunished crime

## Read the Full Text

The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could; but when he ventured upon insult, I vowed revenge. You, who so well know the nature of my soul, will not suppose, however, that I gave utterance to a threat. At length I would be avenged; this was a point definitively settled—but the very definitiveness with which it was resolved precluded the idea of risk. I must not only punish, but punish with impunity. A wrong is unredressed when retribution overtakes its redresser. It is equally unredressed when the avenger fails to make himself felt as such to him who has done the wrong.

It must be understood that neither by word nor deed had I given Fortunato cause to doubt my good will. I continued, as was my wont, to smile in his face, and he did not perceive that my smile now was at the thought of his immolation.

He had a weak point—this Fortunato—although in other regards he was a man to be respected and even feared. He prided himself on his connoisseurship in wine. Few Italians have the true virtuoso spirit. For the most part their enthusiasm is adopted to suit the time and opportunity—to practise imposture upon the British and Austrian millionaires. In painting and gemmary Fortunato, like his countrymen, was a quack—but in the matter of old wines he was sincere. In this respect I did not differ from him materially; I was skilful in the Italian vintages myself, and bought largely whenever I could.

It was about dusk, one evening during the supreme madness of the carnival season, that I encountered my friend. He accosted me with excessive warmth, for he had been drinking much. The man wore motley. He had on a tight-fitting parti-striped dress, and his head was surmounted by the conical cap and bells. I was so pleased to see him that I thought I should never have done wringing his hand.

I said to him—“My dear Fortunato, you are luckily met. How remarkably well you are looking to-day! But I have received a pipe of what passes for Amontillado, and I have my doubts.”

“How?” said he. “Amontillado? A pipe? Impossible! And in the middle of the carnival!”

“I have my doubts,” I replied; “and I was silly enough to pay the full Amontillado price without consulting you in the matter. You were not to be found, and I was fearful of losing a bargain.”

“Amontillado!”

“I have my doubts.”

“Amontillado!”

“And I must satisfy them.”

“Amontillado!”

“As you are engaged, I am on my way to Luchesi. If any one has a critical turn, it is he. He will tell me—”

“Luchesi cannot tell Amontillado from Sherry.”

“And yet some fools will have it that his taste is a match for your own.”

“Come, let us go.”

“Whither?”

“To your vaults.”

“My friend, no; I will not impose upon your good nature. I perceive you have an engagement. Luchesi—”

“I have no engagement;—come.”

“My friend, no. It is not the engagement, but the severe cold with which I perceive you are afflicted. The vaults are insufferably damp. They are encrusted with nitre.”

“Let us go, nevertheless. The cold is merely nothing. Amontillado! You have been imposed upon. And as for Luchesi, he cannot distinguish Sherry from Amontillado.”

Thus speaking, Fortunato possessed himself of my arm. Putting on a mask of black silk, and drawing a roquelaire closely about my person, I suffered him to hurry me to my palazzo.

There were no attendants at home; they had absconded to make merry in honor of the time. I had told them that I should not return until the morning, and had given them explicit orders not to stir from the house. These orders were sufficient, I well knew, to insure their immediate disappearance, one and all, as soon as my back was turned.

I took from their sconces two flambeaux, and giving one to Fortunato, bowed him through several suites of rooms to the archway that led into the vaults. I passed down a long and winding staircase, requesting him to be cautious as he followed. We came at length to the foot of the descent, and stood together on the damp ground of the catacombs of the Montresors.

The gait of my friend was unsteady, and the bells upon his cap jingled as he strode.

“The pipe,” said he.

“It is farther on,” said I; “but observe the white web-work which gleams from these cavern walls.”

He turned towards me, and looked into my eyes with two filmy orbs that distilled the rheum of intoxication.

“Nitre?” he asked, at length.

“Nitre,” I replied. “How long have you had that cough?”

“Ugh! ugh! ugh!—ugh! ugh! ugh!—ugh! ugh! ugh!—ugh! ugh! ugh!—ugh! ugh! ugh!”

My poor friend found it impossible to reply for many minutes.

“It is nothing,” he said, at last.

“Come,” I said, with decision, “we will go back; your health is precious. You are rich, respected, admired, beloved; you are happy, as once I was. You are a man to be missed. For me it is no matter. We will go back; you will be ill, and I cannot be responsible. Besides, there is Luchesi—”

“Enough,” he said; “the cough is a mere nothing; it will not kill me. I shall not die of a cough.”

“True—true,” I replied; “and, indeed, I had no intention of alarming you unnecessarily—but you should use all proper caution. A draught of this Medoc will defend us from the damps.”

Here I knocked off the neck of a bottle which I drew from a long row of its fellows that lay upon the mould.

“Drink,” I said, presenting him the wine.

He raised it to his lips with a leer. He paused and nodded to me familiarly, while his bells jingled.

“I drink,” he said, “to the buried that repose around us.”

“And I to your long life.”

He again took my arm, and we proceeded.

“These vaults,” he said, “are extensive.”

“The Montresors,” I replied, “were a great and numerous family.”

“I forget your arms.”

“A huge human foot d’or, in a field azure; the foot crushes a serpent rampant whose fangs are imbedded in the heel.”

“And the motto?”

“Nemo me impune lacessit.”

“Good!” he said.

The wine sparkled in his eyes and the bells jingled. My own fancy grew warm with the Medoc. We had passed through walls of piled bones, with casks and puncheons intermingling, into the inmost recesses of the catacombs. I paused again, and this time I made bold to seize Fortunato by an arm above the elbow.

“The nitre!” I said; “see, it increases. It hangs like moss upon the vaults. We are below the river’s bed. The drops of moisture trickle among the bones. Come, we will go back ere it is too late. Your cough—”

“It is nothing,” he said; “let us go on. But first, another draught of the Medoc.”

I broke and reached him a flaçon of De Grâve. He emptied it at a breath. His eyes flashed with a fierce light. He laughed and threw the bottle upwards with a gesticulation I did not understand.

I looked at him in surprise. He repeated the movement—a grotesque one.

“You do not comprehend?” he said.

“Not I,” I replied.

“Then you are not of the brotherhood.”

“How?”

“You are not of the masons.”

“Yes, yes,” I said, “yes, yes.”

“You? Impossible! A mason?”

“A mason,” I replied.

“A sign,” he said.

“It is this,” I answered, producing a trowel from beneath the folds of my roquelaire.

“You jest,” he exclaimed, recoiling a few paces. “But let us proceed to the Amontillado.”

“Be it so,” I said, replacing the tool beneath the cloak, and again offering him my arm. He leaned upon it heavily. We continued our route in search of the Amontillado. We passed through a range of low arches, descended, passed on, and descending again, arrived at a deep crypt, in which the foulness of the air caused our flambeaux rather to glow than flame.

At the most remote end of the crypt there appeared another less spacious. Its walls had been lined with human remains, piled to the vault overhead, in the fashion of the great catacombs of Paris. Three sides of this interior crypt were still ornamented in this manner. From the fourth the bones had been thrown down, and lay promiscuously upon the earth, forming at one point a mound of some size. Within the wall thus exposed by the displacing of the bones, we perceived a still interior recess, in depth about four feet, in width three, in height six or seven. It seemed to have been constructed for no especial use within itself, but formed merely the interval between two of the colossal supports of the roof of the catacombs, and was backed by one of their circumscribing walls of solid granite.

It was in vain that Fortunato, uplifting his dull torch, endeavored to pry into the depth of the recess. Its termination the feeble light did not enable us to see.

“Proceed,” I said; “herein is the Amontillado. As for Luchesi—”

“He is an ignoramus,” interrupted my friend, as he stepped unsteadily forward, while I followed immediately at his heels. In an instant he had reached the extremity of the niche, and finding his progress arrested by the rock, stood stupidly bewildered. A moment more and I had fettered him to the granite. In its surface were two iron staples, distant from each other about two feet, horizontally. From one of these depended a short chain, from the other a padlock. Throwing the links about his waist, it was but the work of a few seconds to secure it. He was too much astounded to resist. Withdrawing the key I stepped back from the recess.

“Pass your hand,” I said, “over the wall; you cannot help feeling the nitre. Indeed it is very damp. Once more let me implore you to return. No? Then I must positively leave you. But I must first render you all the little attentions in my power.”

“The Amontillado!” ejaculated my friend, not yet recovered from his astonishment.

“True,” I replied; “the Amontillado.”

As I said these words I busied myself among the pile of bones of which I have before spoken. Throwing them aside, I soon uncovered a quantity of building stone and mortar. With these materials and with the aid of my trowel, I began vigorously to wall up the entrance of the niche.

I had scarcely laid the first tier of the masonry when I discovered that the intoxication of Fortunato had in a great measure worn off. The earliest indication I had of this was a low moaning cry from the depth of the recess. It was not the cry of a drunken man. There was then a long and obstinate silence. I laid the second tier, and the third, and the fourth; and then I heard the furious vibrations of the chain. The noise lasted for several minutes, during which, that I might hearken to it with the more satisfaction, I ceased my labors and sat down upon the bones. When at last the clanking subsided, I resumed the trowel, and finished without interruption the fifth, the sixth, and the seventh tier. The wall was now nearly upon a level with my breast. I again paused, and holding the flambeaux over the mason-work, threw a few feeble rays upon the figure within.

A succession of loud and shrill screams, bursting suddenly from the throat of the chained form, seemed to thrust me violently back. For a brief moment I hesitated—I trembled. Unsheathing my rapier, I began to grope with it about the recess: but the thought of an instant reassured me. I placed my hand upon the solid fabric of the catacombs, and felt satisfied. I reapproached the wall. I replied to the yells of him who clamored. I re-echoed—I aided—I surpassed them in volume and in strength. I did this, and the clamorer grew still.

It was now midnight, and my task was drawing to a close. I had completed the eighth, the ninth, and the tenth tier. I had finished a portion of the last and the eleventh; there remained but a single stone to be fitted and plastered in. I struggled with its weight; I placed it partially in its destined position. But now there came from out the niche a low laugh that erected the hairs upon my head. It was succeeded by a sad voice, which I had difficulty in recognising as that of the noble Fortunato. The voice said—

“Ha! Ha! ha!—he! he!—a very good joke indeed—an excellent jest. We will have many a rich laugh about it at the palazzo—he! he! he!—over our wine—he! he! he!”

“The Amontillado!” I said.

“He! he! he!—he! he! he!—yes, the Amontillado. But is it not getting late? Will not they be awaiting us at the palazzo, the Lady Fortunato and the rest? Let us be gone.”

“Yes,” I said, “let us be gone.”

“For the love of God, Montresor!”

“Yes,” I said, “for the love of God!”

But to these words I hearkened in vain for a reply. I grew impatient. I called aloud—

“Fortunato!”

No answer. I called again—

“Fortunato!”

No answer still. I thrust a torch through the remaining aperture and let it fall within. There came forth in return only a jingling of the bells. My heart grew sick—on account of the dampness of the catacombs. I hastened to make an end of my labor. I forced the last stone into its position; I plastered it up. Against the new masonry I re-erected the old rampart of bones. For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them. In páce requiescat!

## Author

Summary: Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849). This story was written during a prolific period of his career refining his theory of the short story.

Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849). This story was written a few years before "Annabel Lee" (1846 vs. 1849), during the same late, prolific period of his career when he was refining his theory of the short story: that every sentence should serve a single, unified emotional effect (in this case, dread and psychological horror rather than grief).

## Time, Era, and Societal Context

Summary: Poe deliberately sets the story in the past and abroad during Carnival season, a common Gothic technique.

Setting: An unnamed Italian city during Carnival season. Poe deliberately sets it in the past and abroad, a common Gothic technique that let 19th-century American writers explore violence and moral decay at a "safe" historical and cultural distance.

Carnival culture: Real Italian carnivals involved masks, costumes, confetti, heavy drinking, and general chaos — the perfect cover for Montresor to lead Fortunato away unnoticed.

Secret societies: The story includes a brief exchange about Freemasonry. American suspicion of the Masons peaked in the late 1820s and 1830s, but by 1846 it was fading. Poe uses it as dramatic irony (Fortunato assumes Montresor is a fellow Mason; Montresor's real "membership" is as a stonemason, since he's about to wall Fortunato in).

Poe's own life: Written during a period of professional rivalries and personal loss. The leading Poe editor, Thomas Ollive Mabbott, notes that a bitter 1846 quarrel between Poe and the cohorts of Thomas Dunn English and Hiram Fuller likely fueled the story's cold cruelty.

Real-life inspiration: Mabbott notes that Poe's immediate source for the walled-up victim was likely "A Man Built in a Wall" by Joel T. Headley, a traveler's account of a skeleton found inside an Italian church wall.

## Summary (Characters, Setting, Plot)

Summary: Montresor lures his enemy Fortunato into the catacombs with the promise of rare wine, and walls him up alive.

Characters:

Montresor: The narrator. A proud nobleman driven to murder by a series of "injuries" and one final "insult" from Fortunato (never specified). Calm, calculating, and remorseless.

Fortunato: Montresor's acquaintance, a wealthy wine connoisseur with one major weakness: pride. Ironically, his name means "the fortunate one."

Luchesi: A rival wine expert mentioned but never seen; Montresor uses him as a psychological trigger to make Fortunato insist on going to taste the wine himself.

Setting: An unnamed Italian city during Carnival, moving from the noisy streets down into the cold, damp catacombs beneath the Montresor family palace.

Plot: Montresor tells us he has vowed revenge on Fortunato for an unspecified insult. During Carnival, he approaches the already-drunk Fortunato and tells him he has acquired a cask of rare Amontillado sherry but is unsure if it's genuine — and mentions he was about to ask Luchesi to verify it instead. Fortunato's pride is provoked; he insists on tasting it himself. Montresor leads him down into the family catacombs, plying him with more wine along the way. At a small crypt, Montresor suddenly chains Fortunato to the wall and begins bricking him into a niche, ignoring his cries and pleas. Fifty years later, Montresor tells us, no one has disturbed the bones. "In pace requiescat" — may he rest in peace.

## Analysis (Salient Points)

Summary: Poe builds the story around unity of effect, driving toward one feeling: creeping dread, narrated by an unreliable narrator.

Unity of effect: Nothing is wasted; every detail (the carnival, the wine, the descent, the coat of arms) drives toward one feeling: creeping dread.

Unreliable narrator: The story is narrated entirely by Montresor, fifty years after the fact. We never learn what the "thousand injuries" or the final "insult" actually were, forcing the reader to question whether Montresor's grievance is exaggerated. (Mabbott even hints that the mention of "the lady Fortunato" suggests a woman may have been involved).

Dramatic irony: We know Montresor wants revenge from the first line, but not how he'll carry it out. Fortunato remains completely unaware, so every friendly toast reads as savage mockery.

The ending: The ending complicates any easy sense of triumph. Montresor insists true revenge should not "overtake" the avenger, yet the fact that he can recall every detail fifty years later suggests the murder never actually gave him peace. Mabbott argues this makes the story perhaps the most moral of Poe's tales: the murderer at the end remembers that his victim rests in peace, something the criminal has been unable to do for fifty years.

## Major Themes and Motifs

Summary: Revenge, pride, deception, and descent into darkness are the major themes.

Revenge and its true cost: Montresor's opening vow; his claim that revenge must go unpunished and be known to the victim; his still-vivid memory 50 years later.

Pride as a fatal weakness: Fortunato's repeated boasting about his wine expertise, which Montresor weaponizes.

Deception / duplicity: Montresor's friendly manner masking murderous intent throughout; the literal and figurative masks of Carnival.

Descent into darkness: The literal descent from the bright carnival street into the dark, damp catacombs mirrors Montresor's descent into cold-blooded murder.

Alcohol as motif: Wine appears repeatedly (Amontillado, Médoc, De Grâve) — it loosens Fortunato's guard and underscores his lack of self-control.

## Major Symbols

Summary: The jester costume, the coat of arms, the catacombs, and the trowel are key symbols.

Fortunato's jester costume: Marks him as "the fool" of the story — someone easily manipulated, whose good fortune is about to be reversed.

Montresor's coat of arms (a golden foot crushing a serpent that is biting the heel) and family motto, "Nemo me impune lacessit" ("No one attacks me with impunity"): Symbolizes Montresor's family code of vengeance — but ironically, the serpent still bites the heel even as it's crushed.

The catacombs / niter-crusted walls: Symbolize both literal death (ancestral bones) and the buried, decaying state of Montresor's own conscience.

The trowel: A symbol of Montresor's true, hidden identity — not a Mason in the fraternal sense, but literally the "mason" who will wall Fortunato up.

The carnival mask: A symbol of concealment and duplicity — the surface gaiety of the festival hiding Montresor's true, murderous purpose.

## Important Conventions

Summary: The story utilizes classical unities, Gothic elements, and dramatic irony.

"The Cask of Amontillado" is the work where the drama conventions fit best.

Classical unities (loosely kept): The time is one evening during Carnival. The place is mostly the Montresor vaults. The action is one plot, the revenge.

Noble birth: Montresor comes from an old family with a coat of arms and a motto. He tells Fortunato, "The Montresors were a great and numerous family."

Tragic flaw: Fortunato's pride in his wine expertise is what leads him to his death.

Free will: Fortunato keeps choosing to go deeper, even when Montresor offers (falsely) to turn back.

Gothic: It has catacombs, bones, darkness, a decaying aristocratic family, and live burial.

Unity of effect: This is Poe's short story rule: every detail builds toward one single feeling, which here is dread.

First-person confessional narrator: The murderer tells his own story fifty years later.

Dramatic irony: The reader knows more than Fortunato does.

## How the Text Resonates Today

Summary: The core idea that an unaddressed grudge can calcify into obsession still describes real interpersonal conflicts.

The core idea — that an unaddressed grudge can quietly calcify into obsession — still describes real patterns in interpersonal conflict, workplace resentment, and larger-scale conflicts driven by a desire for retribution.

Modern readers often connect the story's central tension (cold, calculated cruelty hidden behind a friendly face) to present-day discussions of manipulation and duplicity in relationships.

Fortunato's downfall through vanity and competitiveness (his rivalry with Luchesi) still mirrors how pride and ego can be turned into an easy weakness for others to exploit.

# I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud
A poem by William Wordsworth
1807 · A memory that dances still

## Read the Poem

I wandered lonely as a cloud
That floats on high o'er vales and hills,
When all at once I saw a crowd,
A host, of golden daffodils;
Beside the lake, beneath the trees,
Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Continuous as the stars that shine
And twinkle on the milky way,
They stretched in never-ending line
Along the margin of a bay:
Ten thousand saw I at a glance,
Tossing their heads in sprightly dance.

The waves beside them danced; but they
Out-did the sparkling waves in glee:
A poet could not but be gay,
In such a jocund company:
I gazed—and gazed—but little thought
What wealth the show to me had brought:

For oft, when on my couch I lie
In vacant or in pensive mood,
They flash upon that inward eye
Which is the bliss of solitude;
And then my heart with pleasure fills,
And dances with the daffodils.

## Author

Summary: William Wordsworth was an English poet and one of the founders of the Romantic movement in British literature.

William Wordsworth (1770–1850) was an English poet and one of the founders of the Romantic movement in British literature, born in the Lake District of England. In 1798, with his close friend Samuel Taylor Coleridge, he published Lyrical Ballads, widely seen as the book that launched English Romanticism. He later served as England's Poet Laureate from 1843 until his death. He lost both parents by age 13 and eventually settled back in the Lake District, living for decades alongside his sister Dorothy, whose journals directly influenced his poetry.

## Time, Era, and Societal Context

Summary: English Romanticism reacted against formal, ornate poetic diction and favored natural, accessible speech.

Literary movement: English Romanticism, which reacted against the formal, ornate poetic diction of the 18th century. In the preface to Lyrical Ballads, Wordsworth argued poetry should use "the real language of men" — natural, accessible speech rather than performed elegance.

Historical backdrop: The poem was composed in 1804 (published 1807, revised in 1815), during the early Industrial Revolution — a period of rapid urbanization that was pulling people away from rural life and, in Wordsworth's view, from a healthy relationship with nature.

The real event behind the poem: On April 15, 1802, Wordsworth and his sister Dorothy were walking around Glencoyne Bay at Ullswater in the Lake District when they came across a long stretch of daffodils. Dorothy recorded the scene vividly in her journal, and William drew directly on her phrasing and imagery when he wrote the poem two years later. His wife, Mary Hutchinson, is credited with contributing the famous "inward eye" line.

The 1815 revision: When arranging his works in 1815, Wordsworth added the second stanza (the Milky Way stanza), added a stanza between the first and second, and changed "laughing" to "jocund". The final stanza was left untouched and was already in the 1807 version.

## Summary (Characters, Setting, Plot)

Summary: The speaker is a solitary wanderer, though Wordsworth was accompanied by his sister Dorothy in real life.

Speaker: A solitary wanderer (a stand-in for the poet), though in real life Wordsworth was not alone — his sister Dorothy was with him.

Setting: The Lake District countryside, beside a lake, among hills and trees.

Plot: The speaker describes wandering alone, "lonely as a cloud" drifting over valleys and hills, when he suddenly comes upon a huge crowd of golden daffodils beside a lake, fluttering and dancing in the breeze. He compares their number to the endless stars of the Milky Way, stretching in a never-ending line along the shore. Even the nearby waves dance, but the daffodils outdo them in glee. In the final stanza, the poem shifts to the present: whenever he lies on his couch in an empty or thoughtful mood, the memory of the daffodils "flashes upon that inward eye which is the bliss of solitude," and his heart fills with pleasure and dances along with them.

## Analysis (Salient Points)

Summary: The poem exemplifies Wordsworth’s definition of poetry as powerful feeling recollected in tranquillity.

The poem is a textbook example of Wordsworth's own definition of poetry: "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings," which "takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquility." The poem isn't really about the moment of seeing the daffodils — it's about what happens later, when memory brings that moment back.

The speaker's "loneliness" at the start is not the anxious loneliness of isolation, but a receptive, meditative solitude — one that leaves him open to being struck by nature's beauty. By the end, that loneliness has been transformed into "the bliss of solitude."

Wordsworth intentionally removes his real-life companion (Dorothy) from the poem, making the speaker's transformation from isolation to joy feel more universal and personal to any solitary reader.

The poem values ordinary, everyday nature (a stretch of wild daffodils, not some grand mythic landscape) as a source of profound emotional and even spiritual nourishment.

## Major Themes and Motifs

Summary: Nature offers joy and healing, while memory allows that experience to endure.

Nature as a source of joy and healing: The escalating, almost overwhelming imagery of the daffodils' number and movement.

Memory and the "inward eye": The shift from past-tense narration to present-tense recollection in the final stanza.

Solitude, reframed as a gift rather than a burden: Opens with "lonely," ends with "bliss of solitude."

Nature and the human spirit in harmony: Repeated personification of the daffodils as dancing companions, rather than passive scenery.

Key recurring motif — dance: The daffodils dance, the waves dance, and finally the speaker's own heart dances — nature's motion becomes internalized as the speaker's emotional state.

## Major Symbols

Summary: The cloud symbolizes detached, drifting solitude—floating above the world and observing rather than participating.

The cloud: Symbolizes detached, drifting solitude — floating above the world, observing rather than participating.

The daffodils: Symbolize joy, vitality, and the abundance nature freely offers to anyone who pays attention.

The stars/Milky Way comparison: Symbolizes something vast, continuous, and almost infinite — elevating a simple field of flowers to a cosmic scale.

The "inward eye": A symbol for memory and imagination — the mind's ability to relive and re-experience beauty long after the original moment has passed.

The couch: Symbolizes a return to ordinary, even weary, daily life — against which the memory of the daffodils becomes an escape or a lift.

## Important Conventions

Summary: The drama conventions do not apply to this poem; it has no conflict, hero, or fall.

The drama conventions (classical unities, noble birth, tragic flaw, free will) do not apply to this poem. It has no conflict, no hero, and no fall.

Instead, it uses the conventions of a Romantic lyric:

Emotion recollected in tranquillity: This was Wordsworth's own definition of poetry. In his preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800), Wordsworth said that poetry results from "emotion recollected in tranquillity," and in the fourth stanza the poet, in tranquillity, recollects an earlier experience.

Nature as a source of feeling and renewal: Everyday nature is the subject, not a grand or mythic landscape.

First-person lyric "I": The poem is about a personal experience told in the poet's own voice.

Plain language: The words are close to everyday speech, which is what the Lyrical Ballads preface called for.

Personification of nature: The daffodils "dance," "toss their heads" and show "glee."

## How the Text Resonates Today

Summary: Modern readers connect the poem’s remembered encounter with nature to mindfulness and emotional well-being.

The poem is frequently cited in modern psychology and mental-health writing as an early literary example of what we would now call mindfulness or the therapeutic use of memory — recalling a genuinely happy sensory memory to lift a low mood.

Wordsworth's general outlook anticipated 19th-century "nature cures" adopted by upscale asylums, which added tranquil landscapes to their therapeutic regimens.

In today's increasingly urbanized, screen-based world, the poem's core message — that time spent noticing nature can leave a lasting, positive emotional imprint — is often referenced in discussions of nature's benefits for stress, mood, and mental well-being. (The 19th-century philosopher John Stuart Mill also famously wrote that Wordsworth's poems in general served as "a medicine for my state of mind" during a period of depression).

Summary: The poem endures because it captures an unplanned encounter with beauty that continues to offer comfort.
